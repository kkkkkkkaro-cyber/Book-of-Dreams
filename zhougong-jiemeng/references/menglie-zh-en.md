## 十梦速查 ｜ The Ten Kinds of Dreams

| 类 | Kind | 含义 | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|

## 第1段 ｜ Section 1

**原文**

凡夢，有直、有象、有精、有想、有人、有感、有時、有反、有病、有性。在昔武王，邑姜方震太叔，夢帝謂己：「命爾子虞而與之唐。」及生，手掌曰虞，因以為名。成王滅唐，遂以封之。此謂直應之夢也。詩云：「惟熊惟羆，男子之祥；惟虺惟蛇，女子之祥。」「眾惟魚矣，實惟豐年；惟旄矣，室家蓁蓁。」此謂象之夢也。孔子生於亂世，日思周公之德，夜即夢之。此謂意精之夢也。人有所思，即夢其到；有憂，即夢其事。此謂記想之夢也。今事，貴人夢之即為祥，賤人夢之即為妖；君子夢之即為榮，小人夢之即為辱。此謂人位之夢也。晉文公於城濮之戰，夢楚子伏己而盬其腦，是大惡也。及戰，乃大勝。此謂極反之夢也。陰雨之夢使人厭迷，陽旱之夢使人亂離，大寒之夢使人怨悲，大風之夢使人飄飛。此謂感氣之夢也。春夢發生，夏夢高明，秋冬夢熱藏。此謂應時之夢也。陰病夢寒，陽病夢熱，內病夢亂，外病夢發，百病之夢，或散或集。此謂氣之夢也。人之情心，好惡不同，或以此吉，或以此凶，當各自察，常古所從。此謂性情之夢也。故先有差忒者，謂之精，晝有所思，夜夢其事。乍吉乍善，凶惡不信者，謂之想。貴賤賢愚，男女長少，謂之人。風雨寒暑，謂之感。五行王相，謂之時。陰極即吉，陽極即凶，謂之反。觀其所疾，察其所夢，謂之病。心精好惡，於事驗，謂之性。凡此十者，占夢之大略也。而決吉凶者之類以多反，其何故哉？豈人覺為陽，人寐為陰，陰陽之務，相反故邪？此亦謂其不甚者爾。

**English**

Of dreams there are ten kinds: the direct, the symbolic, the concentrated, the reflective, the personal, the responsive, the seasonal, the reversed, the illness-related, and the temperamental. In ancient times, when Yi Jiang, consort of King Wu, was pregnant with Taishu, she dreamed that the Lord on High said to her: "I command that your son be named Yu, and I will give him Tang." When he was born, the lines on his palm formed the character *Yu*, so he was named accordingly; and when King Cheng destroyed Tang, he enfeoffed him there. This is called a dream of **direct** fulfilment. The *Odes* say: "Bears and grizzlies are omens of sons; vipers and snakes are omens of daughters," and "Many fish — truly a year of plenty; banners — the household will flourish." This is called a **symbolic** dream. Confucius, born into a chaotic age, thought by day of the virtue of the Duke of Zhou and dreamed of him at night. This is called a dream of **concentrated** intent. When people think of something, they dream of its arrival; when they worry, they dream of the matter. This is called a dream of **reflected** thought. The same event, dreamed by a noble person, is auspicious; dreamed by a humble person, ominous; dreamed by a gentleman, an honour; dreamed by a petty person, a disgrace. This is called a dream of **personal** position. Before the battle of Chengpu, Duke Wen of Jin dreamed that the King of Chu lay on top of him and sucked out his brains — a great evil; yet in the battle he won a great victory. This is called a dream of extreme **reversal**. Dreams in overcast rain leave one oppressed and confused; dreams in sunny drought leave one scattered; dreams in great cold leave one resentful and sad; dreams in great wind leave one drifting and flying. This is called a dream **responding** to the qi of the weather. Spring dreams are of growth, summer dreams of height and brightness, autumn and winter dreams of ripening and storing. This is called a **seasonal** dream. Yin illnesses bring dreams of cold, yang illnesses dreams of heat; internal illness brings dreams of disorder, external illness dreams of release; the dreams of all illnesses scatter or gather. This is called a dream of **illness** (qi). People's hearts differ in likes and dislikes; for one person a thing is auspicious, for another inauspicious. Each must examine themselves, following what has long held true. This is called a dream of **temperament**. Thus: what arises from a prior preoccupation — thinking of it by day and dreaming of it at night — is called *concentrated*. What is now auspicious, now good, and whose ill omens are not borne out, is called *reflective*. Noble or base, wise or foolish, male or female, old or young — this is called *personal*. Wind, rain, cold and heat — this is called *responsive*. The waxing and waning of the Five Phases — this is called *seasonal*. When yin reaches its extreme it turns auspicious; when yang reaches its extreme it turns inauspicious — this is called *reversed*. Observe what ails a person and examine what they dream — this is called *illness*. The heart's likes and dislikes, verified in events — this is called *temperament*. These ten are the broad outline of dream divination. Yet why do so many judgments of good and ill run in reverse? Is it because waking is yang and sleeping is yin, and the workings of yin and yang run opposite to each other? Even so, this applies only to the less extreme cases.

## 第2段 ｜ Section 2

**原文**

借如使夢吉事，而己意大喜樂，發於心精，則真吉矣。夢凶事而己意大恐懼，憂悲發於心精，即真惡矣。所謂秋冬夢死傷也。吉者順時也，雖然，財為大害爾，由弗若勿夢也。凡察夢之大體，清潔鮮好，貌堅健，竹木茂美，宮室器械，新成方正，開通光明，溫和升上，向興之象，皆為吉喜。謀從事成，諸臭汙腐爛，枯槁絕霧，傾倚征邪，劓刖不安，閉塞幽昧，解落墜下，向衰之象，皆為計謀不從，舉事不成，妖孽怪異，可憎可惡之事，皆為憂。圖畫恤胎，刻鏤非真，瓦器虛空，皆為見欺紿。倡優俳舞，俟小兒所戲弄之象，皆為觀笑。此其大部也。

**English**

Suppose one dreams of something auspicious and one's own mind is filled with great joy arising from deep within — then it is truly auspicious. If one dreams of something inauspicious and one's own mind is filled with great fear, with grief arising from deep within — then it is truly ill. This is what is meant by dreaming of death and injury in autumn and winter. The auspicious accords with the season; even so, if wealth becomes a great harm, it would have been better not to dream at all. In general, when examining dreams: things clean, fresh and fine; appearances firm and healthy; bamboo and trees lush and beautiful; halls, rooms and implements newly made, square and upright; open, clear and bright; warm, gentle and rising — such signs of flourishing are all auspicious and joyful: plans are followed and affairs succeed. Things foul, rotten and decayed; withered and parched; tilted and crooked; maimed and unsettled; closed off, dark and obscure; loosening, falling and dropping — such signs of decline all mean that plans are not followed and undertakings fail; uncanny portents and hateful things all mean worry. Pictures and images, carvings that are not real, empty earthenware — all mean being deceived. Actors, jesters and dancers, and the things children play with — all mean being laughed at. This is the broad outline.

## 第3段 ｜ Section 3

**原文**

夢或甚顯而無占，或甚微而有應，何也？曰：本所謂之夢者，困不了察之稱，而懵憒冒名也。故亦不專信以斷事。人對計事，起而行之，尚有不從，況於忘忽雜夢，亦可必乎？唯其時有精誠之所感薄，神靈之所告者，乃有占爾。是故君子之異夢，非妄而已也，必有事故焉。小人之異夢，非桀而已也，時有真祥焉。是以武丁夢獲聖而得傅說，二世夢白虎而滅其封。夫奇異之夢，多有故而少無為者矣。人一寢之夢，或屢遷化，百物代至，而其主不能究道之，故占者有不中也。此非古之罪也，乃夢者過也。或言夢審矣，而說者不能連類傳觀，故其惡有不驗也。此非書之罔，乃說之過也。是故占夢之難者，讀其書為難也。夫占夢，必謹其變故，審其徼候，內考情意，外考王相，即吉凶之符，善惡之效，庶可見也。

**English**

Why is it that some dreams are vivid yet portend nothing, while others are faint yet come true? Answer: what we call a "dream" is a name for a muddled state that cannot be clearly perceived — a confusion borrowing a name. So one should not rely on it alone to decide matters. Even plans made while awake and then carried out may not succeed; how much less can one count on scattered, half-forgotten dreams? Only when there is, at times, a stirring of sincere concentration, or a message from the spirits, can a dream be divined. So the strange dreams of a gentleman are not idle: there must be a cause. The strange dreams of a petty person are not mere wildness: at times they hold true omens. Thus Wu Ding dreamed of a sage and found Fu Yue, and the Second Emperor of Qin dreamed of a white tiger and lost his realm. Strange dreams mostly have causes; few are without meaning. In a single night's sleep a dream may change many times, with a hundred things coming in turn, and the dreamer cannot recount it fully — so the diviner sometimes misses. This is not the fault of the ancients but of the dreamer. Or the dream is recounted precisely, but the interpreter cannot connect categories and read broadly, so the ill omen is not borne out. This is not the fault of the books but of the interpretation. Therefore the difficulty of dream divination lies in reading its books well. In divining a dream one must attend carefully to its changes, examine its signs, consider inwardly the dreamer's feelings and intentions, and consider outwardly the waxing and waning of the Five Phases; then the tokens of good and ill fortune may be seen.

## 第4段 ｜ Section 4

**原文**

且凡人道，見瑞而修德者，福必成；見瑞而縱恣者，福轉為禍。見妖而驕侮者，禍必成；見妖而戒懼者，禍轉為福。是故大姒有吉夢，文王不敢康吉，祀於群神，然後占於明堂，並拜吉夢。修發戒懼，聞喜若憂，故能成吉以有天下。虢公夢見蓐收賜之土田，目以為有吉，因史嚚，令國賀夢，聞憂而喜，故能成凶以滅其封。《易》曰：「使知懼，又明於憂患與故。」凡有異夢感心，以及人之吉凶，相之氣色，無問善惡，常恐懼修省，以德迎之，乃乃其逢吉，天祿永終。

**English**

Moreover, in human affairs: one who sees an auspicious sign and cultivates virtue will surely gain blessings; one who sees an auspicious sign and becomes self-indulgent will see blessings turn into misfortune. One who sees an ill omen and is arrogant and dismissive will surely meet misfortune; one who sees an ill omen and is cautious and fearful will see misfortune turn into blessing. Thus when Tai Si had an auspicious dream, King Wen did not dare rest content in it: he sacrificed to the spirits, then had it divined in the Bright Hall, and together they bowed to the auspicious dream. Cultivating caution, hearing good news as if it were cause for worry, he was able to fulfil the good omen and come to possess the realm. The Duke of Guo dreamed that Rushou granted him land and took it as auspicious; when the scribe Yin read it otherwise, he ordered the state to celebrate the dream — hearing of danger and rejoicing — and so brought on disaster and lost his domain. The *Changes* say: "It teaches one to know fear, and makes one understand calamity and its causes." Whenever a strange dream moves the heart — and likewise with all matters of fortune and the reading of complexions — whether it seems good or bad, one should remain cautious and reflect on oneself, meeting it with virtue; then one will meet good fortune, and heaven's favour will last to the end.
